Recommendation — softened

Flat does read technical. The terminal is cut on the radius, so each arc ends on a slight diagonal, and at small sizes those diagonals look like the artwork was clipped rather than drawn. It is the least forgiving in print and the first to look like a mistake.

Fully rounded is not inherently feminine, but it is inherently soft, and soft concentric arcs with round caps is the single most-travelled form language in wellness and mindfulness branding. That is the risk worth naming — not gender, but category collision. You said you do not want to resemble a traditional wellness brand; full round caps walk straight into it.

Softened is the answer. At roughly a fifth of the stroke weight the corner is off but the stroke still reads as drawn with an instrument rather than squeezed from a tube. It holds the evidence-based, considered feeling you asked for, and it survives scaling in both directions. It is also the detail almost nobody consciously notices and everybody feels.

03

Colour order

Six arrangements on bone. Navy dominant in every one, light blue nowhere.

On dark — secondary

Navy cannot appear inside the mark on a navy ground, so the dark colour way inverts: bone and stone carry the arcs and copper stays the accent.

Recommendation — B, graded navy with copper at the core

You described the ripple as insight, skills and influence moving outward from individuals to teams to organisations. If that is the idea, the innermost arc is the individual and the outermost is the organisation — which means the accent belongs at the centre. Copper at the core is the spark; navy carries it outward. Put copper on the outside and the mark says the opposite: influence arriving from outside and moving in.

The graded navy (B) beats the flat navy (A) because the tonal step gives the outward movement somewhere to go. A is the more austere option and is a legitimate fallback if B ever feels busy.

C, D and E are for elimination. C reverses your own logic. D neutralises the accent by burying it. E shows what the mark looks like with no accent at all — worth seeing once, so the copper has to earn its place rather than just be assumed.

Recommendation — burnished copper, and consider going one step deeper

Dawn amber is out. On bone it reaches only 2.26:1 — below the 3:1 floor for a graphic element. It is genuinely lovely on dark (7.88:1) but you have made on-light the primary, and an accent that dissolves on the primary ground is not an accent. If you fall in love with amber, the honest move is to flip the primary to dark, not to stretch the amber.

Burnished copper #CE7A44 sits at exactly 3.0:1 on bone — it passes, with nothing to spare. That has one practical consequence worth writing into the guide: set the logo on bone, never on pure white, where it drops to 3.23:1.

If you want headroom rather than a pass-by-a-hair, #C06033 takes it to 3.92:1 and still reads as burnished copper rather than rust. My preference is this one — it holds up on white, on a projector, and on a bad photocopy.

05

Does stone belong inside the mark?

Recommendation — no. Stone belongs around the mark, not in it.

Three reasons. First, the mark already carries two ideas — navy for depth, copper for the accent. A warm grey is a third, and three ideas in three arcs means none of them is the point.

Second, stone is a surface colour. Its whole job is to be the thing other things sit on. The moment it becomes a figure it stops reading as ground, and the system loses its neutral.

Third, practically: Stone 600 against bone is a close value, so the middle arc goes muddy at small sizes and disappears entirely in one-colour reproduction. The navy tint holds at every size because it stays in the same hue family as the outer arc.

Use stone and bone for backgrounds, rules, cards, table borders and secondary type. That is where it does the most work — and it is what keeps the brand human rather than clinical, which is exactly what you asked stone to do.

Recommendation — C3 alone is the logo; the tagline is a lockup, not part of it

Treat them as two separate assets. The logo is the mark plus C3. That is what goes on the favicon, the avatar, the deck corner, the sign, the shirt. It never needs the tagline to work.

The tagline lockup is a second, optional asset — mark, C3, and the tagline set on two lines beneath. Use it where the brand is being introduced: a homepage hero, the cover of a proposal, a conference banner, an email footer. Use it once per surface, not everywhere.

That split resolves your balance worry without keeping "Collaborate" in the mark. The horizontal one-line version is the only one I would not build — it is the worst of both.

The domain is a locator, not a name. C3Collaborate.com belongs in the same visual register as a phone number — body type, small, near the edge of the page. Putting it in or under the logo would reintroduce exactly the imbalance you are trying to remove.

08

Do we explain the three C's?

Short answer: yes, but in one place — and never in the logo.

A mark that needs a legend is weak. A mark that rewards one is strong. Nobody has to know what the three arcs mean for the logo to work — it reads as movement outward, which is the feeling you want, and that is enough on its own.

But when someone does ask, the answer should be findable, consistent, and stated once properly rather than half-explained in five places.

Where to put it

  • About page — the full formal name and a short paragraph on each C. This is the canonical source.
  • Proposals and contracts — the formal name on the title page or in the signature block.
  • First slide of a deck — if the room is new to you.
  • Not the footer of every page, the email signature, the business card, or beneath the logo.
09

One thing worth raising

The three C's changed, and it costs the mark some of its story

The earlier proposal was Clarity · Capacity · Culture — individual, team, organisation. That maps onto the Ripple exactly: three arcs, three scales, movement outward. The mark and the words were saying the same thing.

Connect · Communicate · Collaborate are three modes rather than three scales. They are all relational verbs at roughly the same altitude, so the outward progression the Ripple draws no longer has a verbal counterpart. The mark still works — but it illustrates the name less precisely than it did.

This is not an argument to change back. Connect, Communicate, Collaborate is plainer, more immediately understood, and more usable in a sentence — real advantages. It is only worth knowing that the ripple-as-progression explanation gets weaker, so the About-page copy has to do a little more work than it would have.

If you want both, one option is to keep Connect / Communicate / Collaborate as the name and use the individual → team → organisation progression as the description of how you work rather than as the meaning of the letters.

Where this leaves us

The recommended mark

  • Ripple, three arcs, even weight.
  • Softened terminals — corner radius at a fifth of the stroke.
  • Navy 950 outer, Navy 700 middle, copper inner — the accent at the source.
  • Copper #C06033, one step deeper than the current Ember 400, for contrast headroom on light.
  • Stone and bone outside the mark, carrying backgrounds and neutrals.
  • Logo is mark + C3. Tagline lockup is a second, optional asset, set on two lines.
