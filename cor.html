01 · Brand Identity

The month a family’s life turns around.

The assessment put Everyman and Magician one point apart, close enough that we asked a second round of questions rather than guess. Those answers were decisive. Given the choice, you picked transformation four times out of five: what the box should make a family feel, what a member should feel after a year, how you’d want COR described, and which description of COR you’d frame. Magician leads. Everyman is how that transformation arrives, in the hands of ordinary people who could just as easily be the ones needing it.

Magician · The Core
Hard months, turned

A contribution goes in. A paid repair, a covered month, a family back on its feet comes out. COR exists to change what happens next, and to let people watch it happen.

  • You’d reward the people who “turn obstacles into transformative breakthroughs,” the Magician’s answer on a top-weighted question.
  • Asked what a family should feel opening the box: “something good is actually happening. Everything is about to turn around.”
  • Asked how you’d most want COR described: “what they do for families is honestly kind of magical.”
Everyman · The Delivery
Nobody is a charity case

The turn is performed by regular people for regular people. No rescuers, no victims, no distance between the giver and the family.

  • You chose the Kind Neighbor as the character closest to COR, and “down-to-earth and relatable” as its nature.
  • The post that should stop a stranger is the one that looks like their own family, not the one that looks impressive.
  • Your ambassador is the dog, the most everyday companion there is.
Creator · The Standard
Made better than it has to be

The box, the card, the site and the merch are not packaging around the mission. They are how a stranger decides COR is real.

  • Asked what someone should think landing on the site, you chose “this is beautifully done, I’ve never seen giving feel like this” over “my money is in good hands.”
  • Your motto, “if it can be imagined, it can be created,” is the Creator’s, verbatim.
  • “Innovative” is one of your three named strengths.

At the core, you’re a Magician.

The Magician’s drive is transformation: the belief that a situation can be changed, not merely endured. Everything in COR’s model is built to make that change visible. A family opens a box and the week ends differently than it began. A COR Credit goes in and a bill is paid at the source. When we asked what a member should still feel after a year of giving, you didn’t choose belonging; you chose “I’ve watched what my giving makes possible, and it still amazes me.” That is the Magician’s relationship with a supporter: not warmth alone, but wonder. Lean into: the turn, told specifically, with the before and after measured in a week rather than a lifetime.

Everyman is how it is delivered.

A Magician brand without the Everyman becomes a spectacle: impressive, distant, faintly superior. Your own words rule that out. Your audience is “good people who have the ability and desire to help other good people through hard times,” a sentence with no rescuer and no victim in it. Everyone in it is the same kind of person, and some are having a harder year. That is why the transformation can never be performed at a family. The Everyman keeps the Magician honest: same clothes, same neighborhood, same kitchen table, and then something remarkable happens anyway. Lean into: “we” over “you,” members over donors, and families shown as neighbors rather than cases.

Why the combination is one-of-a-kind.

The twelve archetypes group into four families by core human drive. Your two leaders come from different ones: Magician from Legacy, Everyman from Connector. The family carrying the most scored points is neither, Structural at 14 of 37, which is the machinery, the credits, the vendor payments and the care. But when we asked directly, you never once chose the Structural answer. Not “taken care of,” not “in good hands,” not systems over energy. The structure is real and it should be visible in your receipts, but it isn’t the feeling. Hero showed up instead: for a family raising money with COR matching it, you want them to feel “we did this, we fought for our family, and we’re winning.”

The one thing to make unmistakable
Nobody at COR is a charity case, and nobody is a saint. Hard things happen to good people, and good people show up.
“Good people, showing up.”
The identity in one line: ordinary people, turning a family’s hardest month around, in the open where everyone can see it
One fear sits underneath: deceit. It’s the Innocent’s fear, and the only reason Innocent scores at all. It is also the fear your supporters bring to every nonprofit they meet. Paying vendors directly answers it structurally, and the approved 501(c)(3) answers it legally. The brand’s job is to make both visible, constantly, without being asked.

The guardrail: the Magician leads, the Everyman keeps it human, the Creator sets the standard. If a line sounds like a fundraising appeal, it’s wrong. If it sounds like a miracle performed on somebody, it’s worse. You’re the neighbor who changes the ending.

03 · Core Purpose

Turn the hardest month of a family’s life, and let good people do the turning.

COR exists so that when a hard time lands on a good family, a community is already standing there, paying the repair shop, the landlord or the pharmacy directly, and so that the people doing the paying feel like members of something they’re proud of rather than targets of an ask. You said it yourselves: giving back should feel less like an obligation and more like a way of life. The purpose isn’t to collect donations. It’s to build people who don’t need to be asked, and to give them a tangible, transparent way to change a family’s next month.

It’s the Magician’s purpose, this can change, delivered the Everyman’s way: by people who could just as easily be on the other side of it.

04 · Voice & Personality

Speak like the neighbor who already has a plan.

If the brand were a person: the neighbor everyone on the street has a number for. Wears the same hoodie to the hardware store and the fundraiser. When your car dies, doesn’t make a speech about community; texts “which shop is it at?” and handles it. Remembers your kid’s name. Posts the receipt, not the halo. Funny at the cookout, straight with you when it matters, and never once makes you feel like you owe them.

Transformative

We are specific about what changed, forward-moving, quietly amazed  ·  not miracle-talk, hype, or before-and-after pity.
“Tuesday the transmission went. Friday she drove to work. That was you.”

Neighborly

We are plain-spoken, inclusive, first-person plural  ·  not folksy, generic, or “dear valued donor.”
“You don’t have to be a donor. You just have to be one of us.”

Transparent

We are receipts-first, exact, comfortable with the fine print  ·  not vague, defensive, or “every dollar makes a difference.”
“$412 to the repair shop on the 9th. Paid direct. Receipt’s below.”

Hopeful

We are warm, energetic, honest that hard things are hard  ·  not sad-piano, guilt-driven, or toxically positive.
“Hard year. Good people. Better month.”

On the dials: changed over helped · specific over sweeping · hopeful over heavy · receipts over rhetoric · member over donor · wear-it-proud over give-and-forget

The guardrail: never make a family the sad part of someone else’s good story. They’re neighbors having a hard year, and the brand talks about them the way you’d talk about a neighbor when she’s standing in the room.

The identity only matters if it opens a lane nobody else can follow you into. You’re launching nationally, and you told us the real competition isn’t only other charities; it’s every brand competing for attention, loyalty and the desire to belong. So we mapped both.

06 · Differentiation Strategy

Five ways to pull away.

Each pillar targets the open lane, and each is something the brand should actively say and show, not just imply.

Sell membership, not donation.

The platforms and the charities both run on the ask: a need appears, a request goes out, the relationship pauses until the next one. charity: water showed what happens when a monthly gift becomes a named community people join; its monthly programme became the centre of its revenue. Your audience already doesn’t think of themselves as donors, and you’ve named them COR Members. Use it everywhere.

In the brandMake “Become a COR Member” the primary action everywhere, with one-time giving secondary and an annual option beside the monthly. Carry the line good people helping good people, every single month. Retire “donate now” as a headline button.

Make every credit visible.

The COR Credit is the Magician’s device, and a trick nobody sees isn’t magic. Crowdfunding shows a progress bar; charities show an annual report. Neither shows a member what their own month turned into. COR can: which kinds of need were met, how many families, which categories of vendor were paid, and when.

In the brandA monthly “what your month did” report for every member, a running public tally of credits paid, and story formats that always end on the specific thing that changed.

Put the receipts where the halo usually goes.

Deceit was one of your three fears, and your supporters share it. Paying vendors directly is a strong answer, but not a complete one. In 2025 the founder of Modest Needs, the country’s best-known pay-the-creditor charity, pleaded guilty to diverting more than $2.5 million in donations, and federal prosecutors said he had listed people on his board who had never agreed to serve. The payment model didn’t protect donors; governance they couldn’t see failed them. Make the people and the oversight as visible as the credits.

In the brandShow your board and team with faces and real roles. Publish credits paid on a fixed cadence, and your financials as they exist. Treat transparency as content, designed as carefully as the merch, not as a PDF in a footer.

Tell hard stories without the sad music.

The category default is the pity frame: the grey photograph, the slow piano, the before-and-after. You ruled it out in your own words, and the archetype work backs it: the Magician tells a story about what changed, not about how bad it was. That frame lets a family be featured without being diminished, and lets a member share it without it feeling like a guilt trip. It also separates your two events cleanly: one where families, members and donors simply belong together, and one built to send parents home believing something is possible for them.

In the brandA fixed story shape: the hard thing, the turn, the next week. Families shown active, with consent, as neighbours rather than cases. No before-and-after hardship imagery, ever.

Make the merch a membership card, not the funding plan.

Apparel is how COR becomes something people identify with and talk about, and it’s the Creator’s strongest tool in your stack. But merch-funded giving is fragile. LYM, one of the most successful give-back apparel brands, paused operations in 2025 before its founder bought it back and relaunched it. Monthly membership should carry the credits; the merch should carry the flag. The member welcome kit is the other half of this: the tee, the handwritten note and the impact card are what turn a payment into an identity.

In the brandMember-only drops, garments that signal “I’m in” rather than “I bought this,” and an impact card that asks members for a photo and a line about why they joined, so the brand is carried by its people rather than by stock imagery.

Your strategic sweet spot
The neighborhood that changes the ending.

Magician follow-through, Everyman belonging, and receipts nobody else in the category bothers to show.

07 · Visual Identity · Concept Strategies

Three ways to look the part.

Direction, not decoration: three strategic concepts, each true to Magician × Everyman and to your brief for something simple, minimal and classic, with the brand standing back so real photography and video carry the emotion. The wordmark is COR, with no symbol. The designed boards follow once we’ve agreed a direction together.

Concept A
Neighborhood goods

A well-made local label that happens to do enormous good.

Charcoal, cream and stone on heavyweight cotton, uncoated stock and painted signage. The visual language of good workwear and the hardware store, not of charity: sturdy type, generous space, honest materials, photography doing the talking.

Pros
  • Carries naturally across apparel, the box and signage
  • Classic and timeless, exactly as briefed
  • Instantly separates COR from charity visuals
Tradeoffs
  • Can go quiet; leans hard on the quality of the photography
  • Gives the Magician nothing to do on its own
Concept B
The turn

One device that marks the moment things change.

A neutral system built around a single signature: a mark that moves from charcoal into light, or one deep red rule that appears only where something turned. It shows up on credit reports, story endings, receipts and the member card, and nowhere else.

Pros
  • Gives the lead archetype a visible job
  • Makes transparency content look designed
  • Ownable, because the device carries a meaning
Tradeoffs
  • Needs discipline so the device doesn’t become a gimmick
  • Used freely, a light-to-dark gradient drifts toward tech
Concept C
Community bulletin

Hand-made, playful, pinned to the corkboard.

The playful pick in your style list, taken furthest: illustrated icons, patterns, sticker-sheet marks and a looser, drawn feel, built for the iconography, patterns and illustration in your scope.

Pros
  • The warmest and least intimidating option
  • Merch-friendly and made for social
  • Leans hardest into the Everyman
Tradeoffs
  • The fastest to date, and you’ve ruled out trendy
  • Weakest with business-owner supporters who want credibility
  • Fights the minimal brief and loses the Magician

The color thinking

You chose charcoal #3A3E4A, cream #F4EDDC and stone #8A8D96, asked for strictly neutral, and raised one possibility: a deep Americana red. That instinct is right, and the worry about looking like a mall brand is really about which red. The bright primary flag red is the one that reads as discount retail. A deep brick or oxblood, roughly #7A2B2B to #8E2C2C, reads as heritage workwear instead, and against charcoal and cream it gives the Magician the single moment of heat that three neutrals can’t. Use it on almost nothing: the wordmark lockup, one rule on the box, the member card. Two things to steer around. Charcoal and stone at scale, in a hardship context, can turn somber, so make cream the paper and charcoal the ink. And charcoal-with-cream is also the palette of quiet-luxury labels, which would put distance between COR and its Everyman half, so keep the type plain and the photography warm. Your avoid list holds: the purples are the Magician’s mystic cliché, and tangerine pulls straight toward retail signage.

The type thinking

You picked warm and humanist, and the archetypes agree. The Everyman needs type that reads like a person talking: open letterforms, generous spacing, nothing precious. A humanist sans such as Mulish or Nunito Sans should carry the whole system, from app screens to hang tags. The Magician and the “classic” in your brief want one moment of character on top, and a warm, bookish serif like Lora, used for headlines and story pull-quotes, gives it without chasing the soft display serifs that are everywhere right now. Avoid geometric startup faces like Poppins; they read as an app, not a neighbour. Since the identity is a wordmark with no symbol, COR itself should be custom-drawn, wide enough to hold its own on a hat and sturdy enough to embroider.

Our lean: Concept A as the foundation, carrying Concept B’s turn device as its one signature, with Concept C’s playfulness confined to iconography and pattern. Everyman in the materials, Magician in the moment of change, and the photography left room to do the emotional work.

The business
Describe what you do
COR Support is a nonprofit that provides direct, practical support to families going through hard times. Approved families receive COR Credits that can be used toward real needs, with COR paying trusted vendors directly rather than simply handing out cash.

We fund that support by building a community of people who want to help through monthly contributions, one-time giving, merchandise, partnerships, events, and sharing the stories behind COR. People will primarily discover us through social media, word of mouth, community partnerships, events, merchandise, and the families and supporters already connected to COR.

What we’re intentionally building differently is the experience around giving. We don’t want COR to look, sound, or feel like a traditional nonprofit. We want it to feel more like a lifestyle brand and community people are genuinely excited to belong to, while creating a transparent and tangible way for good people to help other good people through hard times.

Who they lose business to
Because COR Support is pre-launch, we aren’t currently losing supporters or donations to specific organizations. We expect to be compared with local and national nonprofits that provide direct assistance to families experiencing hardship, community foundations, mutual-aid organizations, and crowdfunding platforms like GoFundMe.

From a brand perspective, though, we see our competitive landscape more broadly. We’re competing for people’s attention, loyalty, discretionary spending, and desire to belong. We want COR to have the cultural pull and community of a great lifestyle brand while directing that energy toward helping people. That’s the space we believe can make COR feel very different from a traditional charity.
Ideal customer
Our ideal supporter is someone who is doing well in life and genuinely wants to do some good with it. They are typically working professionals, business owners, entrepreneurs, families, and community-minded people with enough disposable income to contribute regularly, purchase merchandise, attend events, or support a family when the opportunity arises.

They value hard work, generosity, transparency, community, and actually following through. They’re the people who would rather help solve a problem than simply talk about it. They spend money on brands they connect with, care about where their money goes, and are drawn to organizations that feel modern, credible, positive, and human.

They may not consider themselves a traditional “donor,” and that’s important to us. We want COR to attract people who want giving back to be part of how they live, not something they only think about when they’re asked for a donation.

Ultimately, our audience is pretty simple: good people who have the ability and desire to help other good people through hard times.
What makes them different
COR Support is being built to feel less like a traditional nonprofit and more like a lifestyle brand that happens to do a tremendous amount of good.

We aren’t interested in building something people only interact with when they’re asked to donate. We want COR to become something people identify with, wear, talk about, share, participate in, and genuinely want to be part of.

The giving model is intentionally tangible, too. Approved families receive COR Credits, and COR pays vendors directly toward the things those families actually need. Supporters can see what their contributions are helping make possible, and transparency and follow-through are incredibly important to us.

Most importantly, COR isn’t built around sadness, guilt, or making people feel obligated to give. Hard things happen to good people. We want to tell those stories honestly while keeping the brand hopeful, energetic, cool, and focused on what happens when a community decides to show up.

We want giving back to feel less like an obligation and more like a way of life.
